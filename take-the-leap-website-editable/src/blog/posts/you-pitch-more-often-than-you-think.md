A few weeks ago I was at the Entrepreneurs Summit at Crown. I wasn't there to pitch. I was there to watch: how the presenters pitched to a huge room, what their decks looked like, and what worked and what didn't.

At the lunch break, a woman sat down near me, noticed my wristband and asked if I was at the conference. When I said yes, she asked, "So what is it that you do?"

Without thinking, I said: "I help startups and founders pitch their ideas for investment. I help them refine their ideas."

She got goosebumps. She told me she was meant to sit at my table, because that was exactly what she'd been looking for. She moved next to me, took my details and asked me question after question.

I hadn't planned any of it. I didn't even realise I was pitching, but I was, and that one sentence did all the work.

## Who are you pitching to? Everyone, all of the time

- **Customers:** how good your product or service is, and what it can do for them.
- **Employees:** if you're hiring, why your business is the best place to work.
- **Investors:** including some you won't even know are investors.
- **Peers and partners:** a co-founder, a marketing person, or the developer who builds your app.
- **Friends and family:** and whoever happens to be sitting at the next table.

You never know who's listening. Someone who overhears you might want to join your project, help you, buy from you or invest.

## Meet Drunk Bob

Here's my favourite way to explain it. You're at a wedding, and Drunk Bob is at the bar.

Never underestimate Drunk Bob, because his wife is a multi-million-dollar investor. Your challenge is to explain your business so clearly that when Bob gets home, he can explain it to her. And she might just decide to invest in you.

The same goes for a 10-year-old. If a 10-year-old understands your idea, you've made it simple enough.

## Cut the jargon and keep it simple

In a room full of people from your industry, you might get away with a bit of jargon. But when you don't know your audience, simplicity is everything.

Confusion kills opportunity.

I recently listened to 20 pitches back to back, and the ones I remembered afterwards were the simple ones. Be the person who stands out because you're easy to understand. Complicated ideas are hard to remember, and jargon makes them harder.

## Start with Drunk Bob, then build up

You might end up with several versions of your pitch: a Drunk Bob pitch, an elevator pitch, one, three, five and ten-minute versions, and eventually a half-hour investor deck.

I always recommend starting with Drunk Bob and building up from there, adding a little more each time. Depending on who you pitch to, you might use four or five of them.

That one sentence could lead to a full investment pitch, or just another cup of coffee. The possibilities are endless once you can explain what you do without confusing anyone.

So never forget who you might be pitching to. They could change your life.
