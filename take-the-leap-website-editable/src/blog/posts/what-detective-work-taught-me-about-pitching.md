For ten years in the police, my job was to get people to tell me the truth: clearly, under pressure, and often when they didn't want to. Interview rooms don't leave much room for waffle, and you learn fast what works and what doesn't.

When I started working with founders, I was surprised how much of that carried straight across. Here are four lessons from the interview room that apply directly to your pitch.

## 1. Lead with the point, not the build-up

In an interview room, you don't get ten minutes to warm up before you say the important thing. The same is true in a pitch. Investors and customers decide whether they're listening within the first thirty seconds. Your story matters, but tell them what you actually do first. If you open with the backstory, you can lose half the room before you get there.

## 2. Watch for the moment someone stops listening

Reading a room isn't a soft skill. It's a practical one. You can see the exact moment someone's attention drops: their eyes glaze over, their posture shifts, and the nodding turns automatic. Good pitchers notice this as it happens and adjust, rather than ploughing through a script whether or not anyone is still with them.

## 3. Clarity builds trust faster than confidence does

People often think confidence means sounding certain. It doesn't. It means being clear enough that the other person doesn't have to work to understand you. Ambiguity, even when it's delivered confidently, comes across as evasive. Founders who over-polish their pitch often lose more trust than those who simply explain things plainly.

## 4. Silence is a tool, not a gap to fill

New founders tend to fill every pause. But a well-placed pause after a key point gives it room to land. In an interview, silence often gets you further than another question. In a pitch, it tells the room that what you just said really mattered.

## The takeaway

None of this is really about pitching. It's about communicating under pressure, which is exactly what a pitch is. The skills transfer more directly than most people expect. Once you see the pattern, you'll notice it everywhere: in negotiations, in team meetings, and when you explain your business to a stranger at a networking event.
